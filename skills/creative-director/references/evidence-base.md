# Evidence base for creative judgement

## Evidence tiers

| Tier | Type | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Peer-reviewed research; large-sample effectiveness databank analyses (IPA Databank); Ehrenberg-Bass Institute empirical work | State as established, cite |
| 2 | Large-scale industry studies with disclosed method (Nielsen, Kantar, System1 with stated samples) | State with source and method caveat |
| 3 | Respected practitioner books and documented case studies (IPA/Effie papers) | Label as practitioner evidence |
| Excluded | Trade-press opinion, agency blogs, award-show claims, uncited statistics | Do not use as evidence |

## Core findings to apply

**Brand building and activation work differently (Tier 1).**
Analysis of IPA Databank cases found that long-term brand building
(broad reach, emotional) and short-term sales activation (targeted,
rational, response-driven) produce different effects over different time
horizons; the most effective campaigns combine both. A rule of thumb of
roughly 60% brand / 40% activation emerged for B2C, varying by category
and context. Binet & Field (2013); Binet & Field (2017).

**Emotional advertising outperforms rational advertising on long-term
business effects (Tier 1).** Emotionally led campaigns in the IPA
Databank were associated with larger and more profit-related business
effects than rationally led ones. Binet & Field (2013).

**Creative quality is a major driver of sales response (Tier 2).**
Nielsen analysis of CPG campaigns attributed the largest share of
advertising sales lift (about 47%) to creative quality, above reach,
brand and targeting. Nielsen (2017). Treat the exact share as
category- and method-specific.

**Creatively awarded work has historically been more effective, but the
link weakened with short-termism (Tier 1/3).** IPA analyses linking Gunn
Report creative awards to effectiveness found awarded campaigns more
efficient; later analysis showed this advantage declined as campaigns
shifted to short-term activation. Field (2010); Field (2019).

**Brands grow mainly through penetration, which requires mental and
physical availability (Tier 1).** Growth comes chiefly from reaching more
category buyers, including light buyers; advertising should refresh and
build memory structures across broad audiences. Sharp (2010); Romaniuk &
Sharp (2016).

**Distinctive brand assets drive correct brand attribution (Tier 1).**
Unique, well-known assets (colours, logos, characters, sounds) help
consumers identify the brand; assets must be both unique to the brand
and famous among category buyers, and built through consistent use.
Romaniuk (2018).

**Attention and right-brain features (Tier 2/3).** Advertising that uses
characters, dialogue, a sense of place, humour and narrative tends to
earn more attention and stronger long-term effects than abstract,
flat, voice-over-led work. Wood (2019); Wood (2021). Based largely on
System1 testing combined with IPA data; treat as strong practitioner
evidence rather than settled science.

**Luxury follows different rules (Tier 3).** Luxury brands build value
through exclusivity, heritage and consistency of codes, and avoid the
comparative and promotional tactics of premium mass-market brands.
Kapferer & Bastien (2012).

## Common myths to challenge

| Myth | Evidence-based position |
|---|---|
| "Target only the loyal core" | Growth mostly comes from penetration, including light buyers (Sharp, 2010) |
| "B2B and considered purchases need rational ads" | Emotional brand building also drives long-term B2B effects (Binet & Field, 2019, B2B Institute) |
| "Constantly refresh the look to stay relevant" | Consistency builds distinctive assets; change erodes them (Romaniuk, 2018) |
| "Activation and performance media are enough" | Short-term effects decay; long-term growth needs brand building (Binet & Field, 2013) |
